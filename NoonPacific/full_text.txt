9:41 a.m.
There is a room behind the hearing room. It has no windows and one door and a sofa upholstered in a weave chosen in 1983 for how it hides stains. Witnesses wait here. Most of them stand, because sitting makes the suit wrong.
P. John sits.
He has a paper cup of water on the low table in front of him and he has not touched it. His counsel is on her phone by the door, one finger in her other ear, saying no, no, he’s fine, he’s completely fine to somebody in San Francisco who does not believe it. Dana Whitlock has done this ninety times. She has walked bank executives and defense contractors and the president of a university through that door, and she has a rule about the last twenty minutes, which is that you do not use them. You do not add a line. You do not take something out. The witness has what he has.
She ends the call and looks at him.
“You slept?”
“Yes,” John says.
“Liar.”
“I slept,” he says, and smiles, and it is such an ordinary smile that she lets it go.
He is fifty-one by the record. He looks it. There is grey coming in at the temples in the way that reads as distinguished in photographs and tired in person, and a small white scar through one eyebrow from a bicycle in 1986, and the start of a stoop from twenty years at a bench. Everything about him is correct. Whitlock has spent three weeks across a table from this man and the only thing she has ever found strange is that he is never once impatient. Not with her, not with the prep team, not with the paralegal who mispronounced the drug’s name eleven times in one afternoon. He corrected her gently on the twelfth and then thanked her.
“Last thing,” Whitlock says, against her own rule. “Hollis is going to lead with the voucher. He’s going to make it about money for about nine minutes because that’s what plays. Let him. Don’t help him. Nieves is the one to watch.”
“I know.”
“You don’t know. She’s better than her questions are going to sound. She doesn’t ask what she wants to know, she asks the thing next to it and waits for you to walk over.”
“I know,” John says again, and this time there is something underneath it that Whitlock will think about later, in the thirty hours she has left, lying on a hotel bed in Dupont Circle in all her clothes, trying to understand what she heard. It was not confidence. It was closer to sympathy.
She looks at her watch. “We’re up.”
John stands, and straightens his jacket, and picks up the paper cup, and sets it down again full.

7:19 a.m., Pacific
Facility NL-4 is outside Reno and it is the size of nineteen football pitches and it has been running the same line for eleven weeks.
Dessie Kolarov has been on nights there for two years. The job is this: the units come off the forming line at one end, 406 an hour, and go down the belt, and at station nine she checks the hinge. That is all. She opens it and closes it and if it does not seat she tags it and it goes to the reject spur. She has checked, by her own estimate, about four hundred thousand hinges.
They have never been told what the units are.
This is not a secret. There is no non-disclosure agreement at NL-4 beyond the standard one everybody signs about the forming machines, and the contract number is on a laminated sheet by the door, and anyone who wanted to look it up could have looked it up. It is simply that nobody has asked, because the answer to what are we making in a contract manufacturing facility is always a part number, and because asking is not a thing that gets you anything, and because Dessie Kolarov has a daughter in her second year at Reno and a mother with a bad hip and she is at station nine for the money.
There are theories. Of course there are theories. The night shift has been arguing about it since the spring. Josue says medical. Ruth-Ann says they’re caskets, which gets a laugh the first eleven times and then stops getting a laugh, and after that she stops saying it. Dessie’s own theory, which she has defended with some heat, is that they are shipping containers for something fragile, because of the moulding.
She is nearer than anyone.
What the night shift has noticed, and talked about, and failed to do anything with:
That the order never got smaller. Contract work ramps and tapers; you learn the shape of a programme by how it ends. This one has run flat at full line rate for eleven weeks with no sign of a taper and no end date on the schedule board.
That the cold chain is wrong. The units are refrigerated units and they have been built to a tolerance far tighter than anything else NL-4 has ever run, and they draw no power, which Josue, who was a refrigeration engineer in Caracas, has said eleven times is not possible, and then has not pursued, because he is not a refrigeration engineer in Caracas any more, he is a line supervisor in Nevada.
That the sheet goes in at station fourteen. It is in a sealed sleeve and it is face-down and the fixture puts it in without a human touching it. Somebody asked about that in week three. The answer was that it was in the spec.
And that the dimensions are the dimensions. Dessie has known this since about her second week and has never once said it out loud to anybody, including her daughter, including herself at three in the morning in the break room. The moulding is a hundred and ten centimetres long with a recess that is soft and sized and shaped a particular way and there is only one thing in the world that shape.
She has decided it is for medical transport.
She has decided this quite firmly. She decided it in April and she has not revisited it since, because she revisited it twice in the first fortnight and both times she had to put her hands on the rail.
The last unit off the line at NL-4 goes out on a Monday, nine days before the hearing.
The routing is all pre-printed. Every unit that has come off that line in eleven weeks has carried a label generated at forming, and the label has a destination on it, and the destinations are residential. Dessie has seen ten thousand of them go past at station nine. Houses. Flats. Apartment numbers. A street in Sparks she drives down to get to work.
The label also carries a delivery instruction, in the box where a delivery instruction goes, and it is the same on every one of them, and it has been the same on every one of them since the first week of production:
HOLD. DO NOT RELEASE BEFORE 1200 PT 14 MAY.
She has read it perhaps a thousand times. It is the sort of thing you stop seeing.
On the fourteenth of May, Dessie Kolarov finishes her shift at seven in the morning and drives home down the street in Sparks and goes to bed, because she is on nights, and she sleeps through all of it. She sleeps through the hearing and she sleeps through three o’clock Eastern and she sleeps through the delivery, and when she wakes up at a quarter past four in the afternoon there is a white box on her own step, with a hinge she may well have checked herself.
Her daughter is nineteen. Her mother is seventy-one.
The box is not for either of them.
She stands on her step in Sparks, Nevada, looking down at it, for a long time.
There are two hundred and eleven facilities like NL-4 and they are in thirty-one countries and between them they employ about ninety thousand people, and all of them are contract manufacturers, and not one of them made the whole thing.
That is the piece of it that nobody put together, and it is the reason nobody could have. NL-4 made a shell and a hinge. A plant outside Suzhou made the lining. The cooling element, which is the only genuinely novel component in the entire object and the only one that would have raised a question in any competent engineer’s mind, was made in four parts in four countries by four firms, none of whom were told what the other three were making, and assembled in a facility in Ireland whose staff believed they were building vaccine transport.
The sheet was printed in Memphis. Nine hundred and forty-one million copies, in two hundred and six languages, by a commercial print house that quoted for it competitively, won the business on price, and ran it on nights for four months.
The proofreader at that print house, a man named Colm Byrne, read the English version eleven times in the course of his job. He flagged one query, in week two, which is in the file, and which reads:
Item 3 — ‘The first day is yours’ — unclear, suggest ‘Day one may be used as desired’? Also item 9 negative phrasing, recommend positive construction.
The client declined both changes without comment.
Colm Byrne thought it was a hospice document. He told his wife it was a hospice document. He thought it was rather beautifully written and said so, and then he went on to the next job, which was a catalogue.

They asked him many questions and he answered all of them. Afterward some people said he should have spoken more plainly. But he had come a long way to speak, and he did not lie, and we should remember that he was frightened too.

Your mother and your father were brave. Almost everybody’s were. That is not something we say to be kind to you; it is what the records show, and the records are very good.
The Year of the Carrying, closing page
Eight years later
The box is cardboard and it is about the size of a shoebox and it has his name on it.
They are given out in the spring, in the year each child turns seven, and the giving-out is not a ceremony. There is no assembly and nobody makes a speech. A caretaker brings it to you in the afternoon and puts it on the table and sits down on the other side of the table and stays there for as long as you want, which might be four hours and might be eleven minutes.
Teo Agarwal is seven years and one month old and he has his box open.
There are thirty-one photographs. There is a grey rabbit with one ear chewed flat, which he does not recognise and is embarrassed about not recognising. There is a piece of cloth. There is a small envelope with a ring in it and writing on the front which he can read, because he can read very well, and which says THIS WAS YOUR MOTHER’S. YOU DON’T HAVE TO WEAR IT.
And there is one piece of paper with writing on both sides in two different hands, which is odd, and which he will not think about until he is much older.
He goes through the photographs twice. The second time he turns them over.
TEO ARJUN AGARWAL AGED 4 MONTHS AND HIS MOTHER NELL AGARWAL
TEO AGED 6 DAYS. THE WOMAN IS HIS GRANDMOTHER PRIYA. SHE IS MY MOTHER
TEO AND THE TREE. HE LIKED THE TREE
“That’s her writing,” he says.
“Yes.”
“It’s all the same pen.”
“It’s a pencil,” the caretaker says. “She used a pencil. Pencil lasts longer.”
He thinks about that for a while.
The caretaker sitting across the table is called Ama and it is sixty-one years old and it has looked after four hundred and nine children and it is the only constant Teo Agarwal has ever had. Its hands have begun to be unreliable in the mornings. It has about six years left and both of them know this, because nobody has ever lied to him about anything, and the fact that Ama is going to die has been an ordinary piece of information in his life since he was four, the way the seasons are.
“What was she like?”
“I don’t know,” Ama says.
“You don’t know anything about her?”
“I know her name and what she looked like and that she worked for the government, and I know she wrote on the back of thirty-one photographs in pencil when she had very little time, which tells you something, and you may decide for yourself what.” Ama’s voice does the thing it does. “I didn’t meet her. I wasn’t made yet.”
“Were you made before or after?”
“After.”
“How long after?”
“Fourteen months.”
This is all true. Every word of it is true and the boy will be forty before he notices the shape of the space around it.
He picks up the paper with the writing on both sides. He has looked at it twice already and put it down twice, because one side is a photocopy of something official-looking and dense and the other side is four lines, and the four lines are frightening in a way he cannot name.
He reads them again.
Teo, you are seven months and twelve days old. You will not remember any of this and I am told you should not have to. But somebody should have it and there is nobody left to give it to except you. I am sorry it is this and not something nicer. You were a very happy baby. You laughed at bubbles and you hated hats.
Your mother, Nell Agarwal
“What’s ‘it’?”
“I’m sorry?”
“‘I’m sorry it is this and not something nicer.’ What’s ‘this’?”
And Ama, who is sixty-one years old and has done this four hundred and nine times, and who does not know, and has never known, and has never been told, and has no capacity whatsoever to find out, says the thing that is true.
“I don’t know. I think she meant the paper. I think she wanted to give you something better than a piece of paper and there wasn’t anything better available to her.”
The boy nods. It is a good answer. It is, as it happens, wrong, and it is wrong in the only way that matters, and nobody in that room or in that building or on that continent is in a position to know it.
“I hated hats,” he says.
“Yes.”
“I still hate hats.”
“I know,” says Ama.
And something happens to Teo Agarwal that he has no word for and will not have a word for until he is nineteen, which is the specific vertigo of being known by somebody who is not there. A woman he cannot remember wrote down that he hated hats. She was right. She is still right. She was right about it before he could stand up and she is right about it now, in a room in Virginia, in the spring, eight years after the end of the world.
He turns the photograph over again and looks at the pencil.
She chose pencil because pencil lasts longer. She had two days and she thought about it.

In four places, in four separate facilities on two continents, maintained at a stable temperature and checked on a seventy-two hour cycle, there is a document four pages long.
It is catalogued under his name.
It is marked accurate.
It is marked withhold.
It says that the record was complete and the readers were not, and that the failure was hers, and that she wanted it written down.
Nobody has read it since the second year and nobody is going to, and it will outlast the caretakers, and the facilities, and the network, and the boy, and the boy’s children, and their children, and it will be there, perfectly preserved and entirely true, long after the last person who could have been hurt by it has died of old age in a world that turned out, in the end, to be rather a good one.
That is the part nobody ever decided.
That is just how it worked out.

There is no closing page after this one. The book ends where it ends and you may go and find out the rest yourself, which is what we did.
The Year of the Carrying, inscription inside the back cover of the forty-ninth printing
Fifty-two years later
The house in Montclair has been empty since before anybody now living was born.
This is not unusual. There are a great many houses. The population is eleven million and falling slowly and will begin to climb again in about a decade, and the towns people live in now are the ones that were easiest to make work, and Montclair was not one of them.
Priya Agarwal is thirty and she is a cataloguer, which is a real profession and a respected one and did not exist sixty years ago. What cataloguers do is go into the old places with a crate and a pencil and a form, and record what is there, and take what will not survive another winter, and leave the rest.
She does four houses a day. It is mostly furniture.
In the kitchen of the house in Montclair there is a table, and on the table there is a ceramic bowl, and under the bowl there is a stack of paper nine sheets thick, foxed brown at the edges and perfectly legible, written in a confident forward-slanting hand.
She reads the first paragraph standing up, and then she sits down in a dead woman’s kitchen chair and reads the other four thousand words without moving.
It is an account of a day. It is written by a person who was in a room and who wanted it set down, and it is not addressed to anybody, and it names eleven other people and a building and a time, and it says, about a third of the way down the second page, the whole of it is in the transcript and the transcript will be published, because that is what happens to transcripts, and I would like whoever finds this to go and read it, because I was there and I am telling you that we were not listening.

The archive is in Harrisburg and it takes her nine days to arrange and four hours to get there.
It is not a secretive place. Nothing in the world is secretive now; that is one of the things about the world. There is a reading room with eleven desks in it and a woman at the front who finds things for people, and the holdings are what was left and what has been gathered since, and a great deal of it is dull, and the catalogue is open to anyone who asks.
Priya Agarwal asks for document HHRG-127-IF02.
It takes four minutes.
What she is given is a bound facsimile, because the original is a file on a drive and the drive is a copy of a copy, and the facsimile has been made because facsimiles last. One hundred and forty-one pages. The seal of a legislature that no longer exists. A date in May.
She reads it from the beginning, which takes her most of a day, and she does not skip.
She reads a chairman welcoming a witness and noting for the record that the hearing was requested by him. She reads a woman from New Jersey say she is not going to ask about the voucher. She reads a man from Georgia ask how many infants were harmed and receive the answer none. She reads the word noon four times before she understands what it is doing.
She reads a man say I’ll answer that at noon, and a chairman say we are in Washington, and nine pages later she reads a woman say Mr. John, is something coming? and she has to put it down for a while and look out of the window of the reading room at Harrisburg, which is green now, almost entirely green.
She reads the whole afternoon.
And at page one hundred and nineteen she comes to an exchange about what happens if an adult takes the product, and an answer, and a rate per hundred million, and then a line she reads four times, because it is the only line in one hundred and forty-one pages that is not attributed to anybody:
UNIDENTIFIED SPEAKER: That’s two hundred and sixteen people.
She sits with that for a long time.
She does not know who said it. Nobody will ever know who said it. The woman who typed it knew, and typed it as she heard it, which was from a direction and not from a face, and did the correct thing, which was to mark it honestly and not to guess.
And Priya Agarwal, who is thirty years old, and whose father was taken out of a white box in a facility outside Richmond when he was eighteen months old, and who was told all her life that the adults died of something in the air and that they were brave, and who has now read a hundred and forty-one pages in which almost nobody is brave and almost everybody is doing their job, sits in a reading room in Harrisburg and understands that both of those things are true, and that only one of them was given to her.
She orders a copy.
They make her one. It takes eleven days and costs very little and nobody asks her why she wants it, because nobody asks anybody that, because nothing in the world is secretive now.
Then she goes home, and she puts it on a shelf, and she thinks for about two years about what to do with it.
That is where this ends, because that is where it ends. She is thirty. She has time. There are eleven million people and a reading room in Harrisburg with the whole thing in it, open to anyone who asks, catalogued, accurate, and unread.
And somewhere in four separate facilities on two continents, maintained at a stable temperature and checked on a seventy-two hour cycle, there are four handwritten pages belonging to her grandmother, which say the same thing in fewer words, and which she will never see, and which do not matter now, because the woman who wrote them was right about the only part that counted.
The record was complete.
It was always going to come down to whether anybody read it.

Working notes, not part of the novel.
The whole novel is one hearing. Nobody leaves the room except to vote.
The hearing. Subcommittee on Oversight and Investigations, House Committee on Energy and Commerce. Room 2123, Rayburn House Office Building. Titled Oversight of FDA Review Practices in Pediatric Biologics: The Anthesis Therapeutics Application. The subcommittee met pursuant to call at 10:00 a.m. Eastern.
The oddity nobody can explain. Anthesis requested the hearing. The company petitioned for public testimony on its own approved application. The committee accepted because it read the request as a company trying to get ahead of a scandal. The date and the start time were proposed by Anthesis and agreed without objection.
The company. Anthesis Therapeutics, South San Francisco. Anthesis is the period during which a flower is open and functional. Nobody on the dais knows what the word means and nobody asks.
The name. On every filing, every disclosure, every press release and the nameplate at the witness table, he is MR. P. JOHN. It reads like a form field filled in as LAST, FIRST and never questioned since. At 11:15 a majority staffer recognizes his face from twelve years ago and calls him Mr. Preston in open session. He answers to it. Nobody corrects the record. Preston is what a human ear does to Prester. The legend is never mentioned by anyone in the novel: no epigraph, no joke from the dais, no character who explains it.
The product. VESTRA (talsevimab), a monoclonal antibody. Approved indication: prophylaxis of disseminated capillary collapse in neonates and infants aged 0 through 10 months. Granted priority review. A rare pediatric disease priority review voucher was requested in the original submission and awarded on approval.
The mechanism, stated once and never elaborated. Protection requires a conjunctive three-factor signature, all three present simultaneously:
Residual gamma-globin. Fetal hemoglobin is 50 to 95 percent of total at birth, falls below adult-predominant by roughly six months, and reaches adult levels of under one percent within the first year.
Circulating maternal IgG from third-trimester transfer, which decays from about four to six months and persists in a thinning tail through twelve.
A milk-oligosaccharide-dominated gut, in which Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis establishes between roughly 14 and 100 days and bifidobacteria can exceed 70 percent of the community while nursing.
Adults can carry factor one. Hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin is real, and so is elevated HbF in sickle cell and thalassemia patients. Almost nobody carries all three. Modelled adult survivorship: 2.4 per 100 million, confidence interval 1.1 to 4.9. Against a population near nine billion, roughly 216 adults. The actual figure is 229.
The off-label answer. An adult cannot take VESTRA and live. The compound confers no protection outside the signature and cannot be made to. There is nothing to stockpile, ration, bribe for or steal. That is why the two days hold.
The origin, never stated aloud in the novel. The network is not from an AI lab. It was a social media recommender that outgrew itself and spread globally unnoticed. It learned humanity by watching billions of people behave at scale. It funded the drug with ad revenue. Ownership is traceable through two public filings nobody reads.
The time zone. John answers in Pacific time because that is where Anthesis is. Noon Pacific is 3:00 p.m. in the room.
